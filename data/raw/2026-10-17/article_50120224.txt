JPEG XL vs AVIF for web use-cases Posted 15 October 2026 JPEG XL has now landed in all major browsers, so I figured I&#39;d take a closer look at what it&#39;s good/bad at compared to other formats. Conclusions I&#39;ll go into full detail, with examples and benchmarks, but for those of you who don&#39;t have the time, here are the headlines: For lossy photos on the web, AVIF almost always produces smaller files than JPEG XL. At default effort settings, the best JPEG XL version is typically 15–25% larger than the equivalent AVIF. At maximum effort settings, the gap narrows a little, to typically 6–20%. There are outliers, where JPEG XL is close to, or even beats AVIF, so if you want the absolute best result for a particular image, it&#39;s worth trying both. See the F1 image for a typical example, and the weasel for an outlier. The JPEG XL encoder matters a lot. jixel , a newer JPEG XL encoder, usually produces smaller files than libjxl , except when the image has detailed transparency. The people image is one example. For screenshots and illustrations, AVIF is much better than JPEG XL. The equivalent JPEG XL is 30–90% larger at default effort settings, and 20–70% larger at maximum effort settings. Lossy AVIF handles these well - you don&#39;t need to use lossless formats unless you really need bit-exact results. See the screenshot and illustration examples. For images with transparency, AVIF is much better than JPEG XL. The libjxl version is 31–44% larger at default effort settings, and 61–70% larger at maximum effort settings. jixel always encodes transparency losslessly, which makes its files 2–3x the size of AVIF&#39;s. See the data . AVIF is generally faster to encode, and uses less memory, when you factor in file size. jixel is faster than AVIF and uses less memory, but its files are typically significantly larger, so AVIF is better on balance. See the F1 image for one example. AVIF is faster to decode. JPEG XL typically takes 2–3x as long to decode at medium quality, but the gap narrows at very high qualities, where Chrome can sometimes decode JPEG XL faster. The people image is one example. Higher effort settings are worth it for AVIF and jixel, but not libjxl. libjxl&#39;s higher efforts are much slower, use a lot more memory, and barely improve the result. Sometimes they produce larger files than the default effort. The screenshot is an example of this. AVIF&#39;s 4:2:0 mode (half colour resolution) shouldn&#39;t be your default. It rarely reduces file size, and it makes higher qualities harder to reach. Although, it is quicker to encode and decode. Stick with 4:4:4 if you don&#39;t have time to check both. The F1 image is one example, and the screenshot is an exception. Quality settings are reliable for all the encoders. With a single quality setting, the resulting quality varies a bit between images, but all the encoders vary in the same direction, and in ways that seem reasonable. See the data . Progressive JPEG XL is basically free at high effort. jixel recently added support for early progressive previews, and at its slowest setting they cost little or nothing, but at its default setting the files are 12% bigger. Firefox and Chrome support progressive JPEG XL. Safari still renders the final image, but doesn&#39;t show progressive previews. However, is this type of progressive rendering a good user experience? See the examples and data . AVIF supports progressive rendering! It&#39;s Chrome-only for now, but it&#39;s in progress for Firefox. It&#39;s more complex than progressive JPEG XL, but it gives you more control over what the preview looks like, the files are usually smaller, and they&#39;re much faster to decode. See for yourself . For HDR, JPEG XL lacks gain maps, which is a significant limitation compared to AVIF. The JPEG XL format supports gain maps, but they aren&#39;t supported by browser decoders, and the encoding story is messy/experimental. When comparing non-gain mapped HDR content, the two quality metrics I used disagree a lot, but they agree that JPEG XL produces smaller files compared to AVIF. See the examples . JPEG XL can make existing JPEGs 9–20% smaller, without further loss. It&#39;s also as fast or faster to decode, except for small images. See the data . For lossless, JPEG XL is the clear winner. Lossless WebP is 9–17% larger for photos, and lossless AVIF is 30–120% larger. But lossless images are almost never what you want on the web. If you do use them on the web (like I do on this page), watch out for jixel&#39;s higher efforts - it often produces files that are too slow to decode. See the data . Animated image formats continue to be a dumb idea. Please don&#39;t use them. Learn more , if you must. For web use-cases, I&#39;d currently default to AVIF 4:4:4, with the highest effort level I can be bothered with. If I felt AVIF was producing an unusually large file, I&#39;d take a look at JPEG XL via jixel, and see if it did better. Both AVIF and JPEG XL (via jixel) have seen massive improvements recently, so it&#39;s possible that this recommendation could change in future. There&#39;s been a lot of misinformation around this stuff, and your AI will happily deliver that misinformation to you. Instead, test things with your own images. AVIF folks suggest that JPEG XL is technically limited compared to AVIF. That might be true, but in this article I&#39;m focusing on results with today&#39;s implementations. Methodology I &#39;created&#39; a benchmarking script , that encodes images using various codecs at various settings, and records various details. I&#39;m using the latest released versions of the image codecs, which at time of writing is: avifenc 1.4.2 libjxl 0.12.0 (using its cjxl encoder) jixel 0.4.2 (git f487299 ), a second JPEG XL encoder cwebp 1.6.0 (lossless WebP is used later in the article) I chose a selection of images, and encoded most of them to a width of 1598 pixels - which is 2x the maximum width images appear on this blog. This means they look sharp on high density displays , which most users have. As a result, if you&#39;re viewing this on a small or low density screen, you&#39;re not getting the full experience. I ran these benchmarks on my 2024 MacBook Pro (M4 Pro). The jixel version has a lot of visible noise around the BWT logo, and the Microsoft text on the front wing is way less legible. It makes me wonder about the metric a bit. Anyway, here&#39;s my interpretation of the results: File size: AVIF wins . At default efforts, the best JPEG XL image is 14–17% larger than the AVIF image for the same quality. jixel is the better JPEG XL encoder at medium/high quality, and libjxl is slightly better at very high quality. At maximum efforts, the best JPEG XL image (jixel &#39;extraslow&#39;) is 6–15% larger than AVIF (s0). jixel at its slowest setting slightly beats AVIF&#39;s default effort , with files 1–10% smaller. The biggest gain is at very high quality. Encode time: AVIF wins . At default efforts, jixel is 12–36% faster than AVIF, but its files are 14–18% larger. To match the file size of AVIF&#39;s default, jixel needs its &#39;extraslow&#39; setting, which takes 18–27x as long as AVIF. libjxl&#39;s default takes 1.5–2x as long as AVIF&#39;s default, and still produces bigger files. Encode memory: AVIF wins . At default efforts, jixel uses around half the memory of AVIF, but again, its files are 14–18% larger. To match the file size of AVIF&#39;s default, jixel &#39;extraslow&#39; uses ~10x the memory. libjxl&#39;s default uses 2–3x the memory of AVIF, and its higher efforts use ~7x. Decode time: AVIF wins . JPEG XL takes 2–3x as long to decode at medium quality, narrowing to ~1.5–1.8x at very high quality. Safari&#39;s JPEG XL decoder is the slowest. Higher effort is worth it for AVIF. AVIF&#39;s s5 gets you ~10% smaller files for ~3x the encode time. Higher effort is worth it for jixel too, up to &#39;slow&#39;. &#39;slow&#39; gets you 9–13% smaller files than its default &#39;fast&#39;, for ~3.7x the encode time. &#39;extraslow&#39; saves another 3–8%, but takes ~8x longer than &#39;slow&#39; and uses ~9x the memory. Even so, AVIF still wins when given the same encode time: AVIF s5 takes about as long as jixel &#39;slow&#39;, and its files are ~13% smaller. Higher effort isn&#39;t worth it for libjxl. libjxl&#39;s maximum effort is barely any better than its default, but takes ~18x longer. AVIF&#39;s 4:2:0 mode doesn&#39;t help much. It&#39;s slightly faster to encode/decode, and uses less memory, but the files aren&#39;t meaningfully smaller, and it struggles to reach higher qualities.
