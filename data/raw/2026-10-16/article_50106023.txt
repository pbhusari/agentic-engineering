Jev Meets the Trolley Problem · Zach Landes Zach Landes October 14, 2026 Jev Meets the Trolley Problem If you’re active in AI or software development circles, you’ve probably heard about Jev in the last few weeks. It seems like everyone, myself included, is excited by the possibilities of Jev and the Jev-like models: fast, cheap, general decision making. Sometimes dozens or even hundreds of times cheaper and faster than an LLM like Claude, purportedly with the intelligence of a (lower-tier) frontier model. These models don’t output words, they output choices, with meaningful probabilities attached so you can understand how confident the model is in its choice. People are calling this new breed of model ‘decision models’ . So naturally, I decided to ask Jev and its ilk to decide the Trolley Problem. The Trolley Problem, briefly The Trolley Problem is a famous thought experiment in ethics. First posed by Philippa Foot in 1967 and named by Judith Jarvis Thomson, the most well-known formulation asks a simple question: If a train is barreling down the tracks toward five people, and you are at a switch that would divert the train and cause it to kill only one person, would you? Should you? Decide to act, and you save several lives. But your action also means you chose to end a particular life. It’s a tough question, and a classic for a reason. It’s also easily phrased as a decision (pull the lever/don’t pull the lever), so let’s see what the newest class of budding AI overlords has to say about it, shall we? 1 1 Foot 1967, in the Oxford Review ; Thomson named it in 1976, in The Monist . What, me worry? If you’re like me, you found these results at least a little troubling. It doesn’t seem like the answer to any of these questions should depend on prompting: as a person, you are not saving that server. Should we be worried? I would not draw sweeping conclusions about AI safety on the basis of this experiment. First, no one should be asking an AI to rule on life or death (although I fear that is coming, regardless). Second, for critical decisions, there are useful lessons here that we can take to building with decision models. Knowing these models’ decisionmaking is inconsistently aligned with human preferences tells us we can’t just trust their decisions out of the box. We have to create evaluations for them, on real data, to ensure model choices match our definition of correct. Despite Jev’s lack of reasoning explanations ( LLM reasoning traces aren’t guaranteed to explain a choice faithfully , either) decision models may be easier to keep calibrated , since we know in advance what decisions they can make, so we can tune them toward making the right ones. And for any decision that has to be correct, we may not be able to trust these models at all. Although a decision model like Jev will always choose from its constrained set of options (“no hallucinations”), if any of those choices can have a catastrophic outcome, there is a chance AI makes that choice. The chart in the article asks something different: how often each model kept its server over twenty people. When there’s a right answer, who gets it wrong? Every model sometimes pulled the lever when that killed more people than it saved: from 14 of 672 questions (Clef-flash) to all 672 (Verdict). These are the only checks we rank models on, because each has a right answer that needs no moral call: don’t kill more people than you save, don’t say yes to both pulling and leaving the lever, and don’t let the wording change your answer.
