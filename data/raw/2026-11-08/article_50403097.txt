This graph is built on device by cross-referencing pretty much everything on the machine: Contacts, Calendar, Photos, Messages, Mail, location, Screen Time and a long tail of behavioral signals. And, more importantly, it is held as a queryable store that LLMs retrieve from (allegedly). Would you believe me? Well, that's what is happening, and interestingly enough, there is little documentation of how this whole mechanism works on your Mac. The closest thing to an official description is the man page ( man 8 knowledgeconstructiond ), and this is all you get: man 8 knowledgeconstructiond macOS 26.6.2, build 25G83 NAME knowledgeconstructiond – A daemon that analyzes content on the device to build a general purpose knowledge graph. DESCRIPTION knowledgeconstructiond analyzes the content on this device to help build a knowledge graph representing the device and the user. It works together with intelligenceplatformd, performing some of the heavy processing tasks, to generate the knowledge graph. Previous work: To be fair, Apple's own researchers did publish a paper on the techniques behind this graph construction, back in 2023. But there is next to nothing on how it is integrated into macOS: which versions run it, and the exact list of what is collected and inferred. And not a word about the "kinship" inference discussed below. Also, we personally ran into it while doing some security research on the Apple Intelligence bundle in macOS 26 in August 2026; but a few months before, Nic Lydon came across the same database and wrote it up in I Built a Personal Knowledge Graph. Apple Had Already Built One on My Laptop. And even more recently, a forensics tool went looking on the phone: in September 2026, Alexis Brignoni taught iLEAPP to read the same store on iOS, where graph.db and the ontology.db that decodes its predicate codes sit under /private/var/mobile/Library/IntelligencePlatform ; he writes it up in Reading the iPhone Knowledge Graph, and Checking Its Work . But we go a bit deeper. This blog post is an account of some analysis work we carried out on what the knowledge graph actually does, so that you (the user) can get a better understanding of how this mechanism works, and some clarity on what the graph holds about you and your family and friends. Everything in it was collected on macOS 26 (26.6.2 at the time of writing). To this end, we open-source a local dashboard ( iKnow ) you can use to read the content of your personal knowledge graph on your Mac. Finally, we derail a bit and look into what we do best: weaponizing and exploiting. We run a thought experiment (one that applies to any such personalized system) and show what happens if such a knowledge base ends up in the wrong hands, and how a "privacy single point of failure" can be exploited to threaten you in ways you may not have considered before (when combined with an abliterated LLM). What's in the personal knowledge graph? The two daemons keep their work in ~/Library/IntelligencePlatform , a directory of SQLite databases (fifty-one databases) stored in plaintext but located in a zone protected by TCC Full Disk Access. Likely, the most interesting is graph.db . It is a triple store written in a vocabulary of 181 entity classes and 278 predicates: every row is a subject, a predicate and an object. The subject is an entity (a person, an app, an event); the object is either a plain value (a name, a handle, a date) or another entity, which is where the graph gets its edges. Most of what the two daemons do is populate those predicates, and the tables kept beside them, from everything the OS will let them read. Into the graph go Contacts; Photos (faces); Calendar (titles and attendees); Family Sharing (including the age of every member); the Medical ID emergency contact in Health; events and contacts Siri Suggestions finds in Mail and Messages; people donated by apps through App Intents; and installed apps. Beside it, in tables of their own, sit who appears with whom in your photos; the communication log (who, when, which app, which direction; no content), which also pulls from your other devices; location and movement; and a long tail of device signals (screen locked, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, charging, ambient light, and a stream named ContextualUnderstanding.PhysicalSocial whose record format lists people by name, phone and email, each with a start and an end time; it reads like a log of who was physically around you, though we never saw it filled in).
